# ICT, Audiovisual and Security Guide

## Purpose and professional boundary

Use this guide for information and communications technology, structured
cabling, audiovisual systems and electronic security across the project
lifecycle.

This guide does not design a network, specify a security threat response,
approve surveillance, determine privacy or records-law compliance, certify
cabling or provide cybersecurity assurance. Obtain project-specific advice from
the ICT, AV and security designers, client technology and security teams,
carrier, legal or privacy advisers, cybersecurity specialists and licensed
contractors as applicable.

## Activation and scope control

Activate relevant sections when the project includes:

- carrier lead-ins, communications rooms, pathways or structured cabling;
- wired, wireless, voice, data or operational-technology connectivity;
- meeting, presentation, hearing, public-address or other AV systems;
- access control, intercom, CCTV, duress or intrusion detection;
- integrations with lifts, fire, BMS, lighting, room booking or process
  systems;
- data-centre, industrial-control or high-availability technology;
- refurbishment requiring cutover, migration or temporary services.

Do not combine ICT, AV and security into one undifferentiated contractor scope.
Identify system owners, specialist designers and interfaces.

## Evidence sweep

Seek:

- user, operator, room and functional briefs;
- client technology, security, privacy and naming standards;
- existing network, rack, pathway, device and coverage records;
- carrier information and landlord or strata boundaries;
- device counts, capacity, performance, availability and retention needs;
- architectural plans, room data, furniture and reflected ceiling plans;
- electrical power, UPS, earthing and metering requirements;
- mechanical cooling and environmental requirements for equipment spaces;
- fire stopping, egress, access-release and emergency-message interfaces;
- vendor systems, licences, warranties and support arrangements;
- operational cutover windows, data migration and decommissioning constraints.

Do not expose credentials, security-sensitive configurations or personal data in
general project artefacts. Reference controlled records instead.

## Functional brief and responsibility matrix

For each system define:

- user and operational outcome;
- spaces, assets and populations served;
- availability, capacity, coverage and performance criteria;
- base-building, tenant, operator, vendor and carrier boundaries;
- passive infrastructure versus active equipment;
- data, network, power, cooling and physical-security dependencies;
- privacy, accessibility, retention and cybersecurity review needs;
- acceptance test and operational owner.

The responsibility matrix should cover design, supply, programming,
configuration, licences, network ports, IP addressing, certificates, testing,
training, warranty and support. "By others" is not a resolved interface.

## ICT rooms, pathways and cabling

Coordinate:

- carrier entry and demarcation;
- equipment-room size, access, fire separation, environment and flood risk;
- rack layouts, power, UPS, cooling and maintainability;
- backbone and horizontal pathways, spare capacity and segregation;
- outlets, consolidation points and device locations;
- fire stopping, labelling, test records and as-builts;
- wireless access-point and coverage planning;
- industrial environmental, electromagnetic or resilience needs where
  applicable.

Pathways and rooms are difficult to correct late. Freeze their design only after
equipment, capacity and growth inputs have been reviewed.

## Audiovisual systems

Translate room use into testable scenarios for presentation, conferencing,
collaboration, teaching, public information, sound reinforcement or hearing
assistance as relevant. Coordinate sightlines, acoustics, lighting, furniture,
screens, speakers, microphones, cameras, controls, network, power and
maintenance access.

Define standard room types and user interfaces where possible. Mock-ups or
bench testing may be appropriate for high-value or repeated spaces. Do not
accept an equipment list without signal flow, control intent and performance
tests.

## Electronic security

Start from a client-approved security risk assessment or operational brief.
Coordinate:

- security zones and access permissions;
- doors, locks, hardware, intercom and lift interfaces;
- CCTV purpose, fields of view, lighting, retention and access;
- intrusion, duress, monitoring and response arrangements;
- vehicle, loading, perimeter and after-hours controls;
- fire-alarm release, emergency egress and fail-safe or fail-secure behaviour;
- privacy signage, governance and controlled access to records.

Do not invent threat assumptions or recommend surveillance merely because
technology is available. Refer legal, privacy and high-risk security matters.

## Networks, integration and cybersecurity

For every integration, define data exchanged, protocol, system of record,
network owner, authentication, timing, fail state, monitoring and support
responsibility. Maintain an interface register across ICT, AV, security, BMS,
fire, lifts, lighting, booking, visitor and process systems.

Cybersecurity should be addressed proportionately through client requirements,
network segregation, secure configuration, credential handling, patching,
remote access, backups, logging and support ownership. Detailed controls and
assurance belong to the client's cybersecurity specialists.

## Refurbishment, migration and live cutover

Survey existing assets, pathways, licences, dependencies and records. Identify
retained, migrated, removed and abandoned systems. Develop:

- cutover sequence, test environment and rollback plan;
- temporary connectivity and security;
- user and operational communications;
- data and configuration backup;
- carrier and vendor attendance;
- after-hours support and acceptance authority;
- secure decommissioning, media handling and records update.

Do not remove a legacy service because the new installation appears complete;
use defined acceptance evidence and operational sign-off.

## Consultant RFP minimum returnables

Request:

- scope understanding, discipline boundaries and assumptions;
- surveys, workshops and user engagement;
- functional brief, room standards and responsibility matrix;
- drawings, schematics, schedules, specifications and interface registers;
- carrier, vendor, client-IT and security coordination;
- privacy, accessibility and cybersecurity referral points;
- tender evaluation, submittal review and site inspections;
- mock-up, test, migration and integrated-commissioning plans;
- asset data, licences, configurations, as-builts, training and support;
- programme, team experience, fee breakdown, exclusions and variation
  triggers.

Require disclosed vendor relationships where they could affect independence.

## Construction, commissioning and handover

Inspect concealed pathways, cable segregation, rooms, racks, outlets, device
locations, fire stopping and labelling before closure. Protect installed
equipment from dust, moisture, heat and unauthorised use.

Commission in stages:

- cable and infrastructure tests;
- device and point checks;
- software and configuration verification;
- functional scenarios;
- coverage, image, sound or performance tests;
- interfaces, alarms and fail states;
- migration, operational acceptance and defects.

Handover controlled as-builts, test results, asset and licence registers,
configurations, backups, warranty and support contacts, training and
cybersecurity ownership. Store secrets in the client's approved secure system,
not in manuals or general project folders.

## PMP contribution

State confirmed functional outcomes, system and information boundaries,
responsibility and interface matrices, carrier and client dependencies,
technology decision dates, pathway hold points, cutover risks, privacy and
cybersecurity referrals, testing, operational acceptance and handover.

## Common failures and low-confidence flags

Common failures are late user briefing, undersized rooms and pathways, base
building and tenant gaps, active equipment procured before standards are
approved, fire and access-control conflicts, unowned integrations, inaccessible
devices, licences omitted, superficial commissioning and credentials placed in
handover manuals.

Flag low confidence when the functional brief, client standards, system owner,
device counts, carrier route, network and power requirements, security risk
assessment, privacy basis, integration owner, cutover window or acceptance
criteria are missing.
