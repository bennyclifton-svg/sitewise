# Mixed-Use Construction Guide

## Purpose and professional boundary

Use this guide as the mixed-use overlay for project planning and consultant
procurement. Mixed-use is a building that combines two or more NCC
classifications in one structure, not a synonym for apartments over shops.
Residential over retail, hotel with apartments, BTR with ground-floor F&B, a
vertical village, and a heritage warehouse conversion have different fire,
acoustic, services, strata, handover and approval consequences. This overlay
owns the interfaces BETWEEN uses. For use-specific depth, consult
`residential-construction-guide.md`, `commercial-construction-guide.md` and
`institution-construction-guide.md`.

This guide is platform guidance, not project evidence. It does not classify a
building or any part of it, design fire or acoustic separation, confirm a
Performance Solution, certify compliance, warrant operator or tenancy
performance, or invent authority acceptance. Those conclusions belong to the
responsible designers, fire engineer, acoustic consultant, certifier,
authorities, contractors, owner, strata and operators. Verify the current NCC
edition, NSW legislation, planning controls, authority requirements, referenced
standards and approval conditions for the actual project.

Common pitfalls:
- Treating mixed-use as a single class, or as apartments with shops underneath
- Using this overlay instead of the use-specific class guides
- Claiming authority acceptance of a mixed-use strategy without written evidence

## Activation and subtype control

Activate this guide when the project class is `mixed` and the work type is
`new`, `refurb`, `extend` or `remediation`. Identify the mix from evidence
before using mix-specific guidance:

- residential over retail (`residential_retail`);
- residential over commercial, including office over retail then apartments
  (`residential_commercial`);
- hotel and residential (`hotel_residential`);
- retail and office (`retail_office`);
- BTR and retail (`btr_retail`);
- vertical village combining childcare, medical, gym, retail, office and
  apartments (`vertical_village`);
- another mix, including heritage adaptive reuse (`other`).

If the mix is missing, further mixed, or novel, record it as an early
decision. Do not transfer fire, acoustic, services, strata or handover
assumptions from one mix to another. A hotel/residential acoustic and
operator-handover regime is not a BTR/retail tenancy-delivery regime.

Common pitfalls:
- Activating mixed-use without naming the mix from evidence
- Copying fire, acoustic or staged-handover assumptions across mixes
- Treating BTR amenity as equivalent to a hotel operator brief

## Evidence sweep

Before writing a PMP or consultant RFP, seek:

- the mix of uses, occupancy models, operator or tenant briefs, and which
  parts are base building, shell, fitout or operator works;
- intended NCC classifications for each part, fire-compartment and acoustic
  strategy, and any fire-engineering or acoustic advice;
- structural scheme, transfer structure at use interfaces, cores, basements
  and shared versus dedicated entries;
- services strategy: separate risers, meters, F&B exhaust, smoke control and
  plant ownership;
- strata, community title, management statement, and whether one
  institutional owner holds more than one use;
- retail shell versus operator fitout, BTR amenity versus retail tenancy
  delivery, and hotel operator standards versus residential standards;
- planning consent, change-of-use conditions and heritage listings;
- staging, trading-continuity and handover dates by use;
- existing-building surveys, structural adequacy and occupied-site rules for
  refurbishment.

Record each source's author, revision, date and issue purpose. Treat an
operator brand standard or a retail tenant's fitout guide as an input requiring
coordinated design, not as proof that the whole building will perform.

Common pitfalls:
- Briefing from a single-use precedent and filling missing interfaces later
- Treating operator or tenant documents as certified design
- Omitting heritage, change-of-use or occupied-site evidence on refurbishment

## Multiple classifications in one structure

A mixed-use building contains two or more NCC classifications in one
structure. Each part is classified separately against the use it serves. Do
not collapse the building to the dominant GFA or to the "primary" use.

Shared structure, shared egress, shared basements and shared plant rooms are
not unclassified leftovers. The most stringent fire requirements typically
govern shared structure and egress. Confirm the governing provisions with the
responsible fire engineer and certifier; do not invent which class "wins".

Three-way mixes (ground retail, commercial office, then apartments) need an
explicit classification map by storey and by part, not a two-use shortcut.
Vertical villages add further classes — childcare, medical, gym, retail,
office and apartments — each with different regulations. Record every class,
the part it occupies, the separating construction, and the independent egress
path.

Change of use, including heritage adaptive reuse, is a classification event.
Do not assume the existing classification or fire strategy remains valid
because the fabric is retained.

Common pitfalls:
- Classifying the whole building as one class because one use dominates
- Leaving shared structure, egress or basement outside the classification map
- Treating change of use as a fitout rather than a reclassification

## Fire and acoustic separation between uses

Provide fire separation between uses as a designed, inspectable system:
compartment walls and floors, openings, penetrations, shafts, smoke control
and the interface with detection and suppression. Separation must continue
through transfer structure, plant, and any shared lobby or loading dock. Do
not describe fire separation as "provided" unless the strategy,
discontinuities and inspection regime are identified.

Acoustic separation is a parallel interface, not a finish item. Hotel versus
apartments, childcare versus residential, gym versus dwellings, and F&B versus
habitable rooms have incompatible source and receiver criteria. Resolve
structure-borne paths through transfer slabs, cores, risers and plant before
architecture is locked. Operator brand standards do not override NCC or
planning acoustic conditions.

Where childcare, medical or other sensitive occupancies sit in a mixed
development, access management and acoustic/fire separation are both
regulatory and operational. Label the strategy low confidence until the
competent consultants and the relevant authority pathway are named.

Common pitfalls:
- Fire-rating the floor between uses and ignoring shafts, penetrations and
  transfer structure
- Using hotel/residential acoustic criteria for childcare or gym adjacencies
- Assuming a Performance Solution is accepted because a similar mix used one

## Stratification, cores and separate services

Resolve vertical stratification early: which uses occupy which storeys, where
the structural transfer occurs at use interfaces, and whether cores and
basements are shared or separate. Transfer structure at a retail-to-office or
office-to-residential interface is a design gate, not a late coordination
issue.

Provide separate entries and services unless evidence shows a shared
arrangement that still meets fire, security, metering, maintenance and
tenancy-independence requirements. Shared cores and a shared basement
concentrate fire, smoke, security, loading and after-hours access risk.

Retail as shell is not the same as operator fitout. Define the shell
boundary, including meters, grease arrestors, F&B exhaust risers, discharge,
make-up air and what the tenant must complete. Reserve F&B exhaust risers so
they cannot re-enter residential or hotel intakes. BTR amenity is part of the
residential operation; do not conflate it with ground-floor retail tenancy
delivery. Hotel back-of-house, loading and staff circulation clash with
residential privacy: decide shared versus separate services against the
operator agreement, not a residential-over-retail precedent.

Common pitfalls:
- Locking the grid before transfer structure and riser reservations exist
- Sharing a core or basement without an access, fire and loading regime
- Omitting F&B exhaust risers, or treating BTR amenity as a retail tenancy

## Strata, ownership and tenancy interfaces

Strata subdivision across uses creates lots, common property, easements,
exclusive-use areas and a management statement that must match the physical
building. Complex three-way mixes need lot boundaries that follow fire
separation, services ownership and access rights, not marketing floorplates.

A single institutional owner holding both uses (typical of BTR with retail)
simplifies or avoids strata subdivision. It does not remove the need for
interface definition. Retail tenancy delivery, BTR amenity, base-building
versus fitout, after-hours access, metering, exhaust and make-good still
require a responsibility matrix. Single ownership can make fitout harder:
the same owner is both landlord and residential operator.

Hotel operator agreements, retail leases, residential occupation and any
building-management committee must be consistent with the same drawings.
Vertical villages produce extreme stakeholder complexity: multiple operators,
regulators, parents, patients, tenants, residents and owners. Name the
governance forum and decision rights; do not leave them as "to be coordinated".

Common pitfalls:
- Drawing lot boundaries that cut through fire compartments or shared plant
- Assuming single ownership makes retail/BTR interfaces disappear
- Leaving operator, tenant and strata documents unaligned with the design

## Staged handover by use

Plan staged handover by use. Different uses reach practical completion,
occupation and operational acceptance on different dates. A hotel operator
typically requires a different handover date, snagging regime, FF&E, training
and brand-standard acceptance than residential apartments. Retail shells may
hand over before tenant fitout; BTR amenity may need to operate before retail
is let.

Do not use a single PC date as a proxy for mixed-use completion. For each use
define the works that must be complete and certified; independent egress,
fire systems, water, power, lifts and waste serving that use; residual
construction access through occupied parts; defects, warranties and manuals
by use; and who accepts — certifier, owner, hotel operator, residential
manager, retail tenant or institution.

Phased approvals follow the same logic. A vertical village may need
childcare, medical or food-premises approvals on a different path and
programme from the residential occupation certificate. Sequence applications
as explicit dependencies.

Common pitfalls:
- One practical-completion date for hotel operator and residential
- Handing over retail shell without reserved risers, meters or fire isolation
- Occupying one use while fire systems still depend on incomplete works above

## Heritage and change of use

Heritage adaptive reuse pits retained fabric against new-use fire upgrade,
accessibility, acoustics, services and structural adequacy of the existing
building. Change of use is the governing event: classify the converted parts
for the new uses, not for the warehouse, office or industrial history.

Do not assume existing structure, floors, stairs, facade or fire resistance
are adequate because they have stood. Investigate, then design. Unknowns in
heritage fabric, contamination, undocumented alterations and concealed
services belong in an assumptions register with provisional sums and
investigation hold points.

Fire upgrade in heritage fabric needs a competent fire-engineering pathway
and heritage-authority engagement. Do not state that a Performance Solution
will be accepted, or that heritage listing prevents a compliant fire upgrade.
Record both constraints and the decision gate.

Common pitfalls:
- Treating change of use as a fitout of the existing classification
- Skipping structural adequacy of the existing building
- Using provisional sums to hide an undesigned fire or services upgrade

## Construction, commissioning and residual risk

For occupied refurbishment, control retail trading continuity against works
above. Offices being repositioned over trading shops need dust, vibration,
water, fire-system isolation, loading, after-hours access and a shared lobby
regime the retailer can operate. Services upgrades through occupied space
require verified isolation, temporary fire protection and daily handback.

Commission by use and by shared system. Shared fire, smoke-control, hydrant,
sprinkler, lift and basement systems cannot be accepted against one use while
another is incomplete unless the staged occupation strategy, certifier and
fire engineer have defined the residual state. Integrated testing must include
interfaces: fire doors between uses, F&B exhaust versus intake, acoustic
commissioning of separating floors, and access-control between childcare,
medical, gym, retail, office and apartments.

State the confirmed mix, classification map, fire and acoustic separation,
transfer and services strategy, strata or single-owner interface matrix,
staged handover by use, occupied-site controls, and unresolved assumptions.
Flag low confidence when those items are absent; do not fill gaps with another
mix's precedent or general model knowledge.

Common pitfalls:
- Office or residential works above while retail trades, with no continuity plan
- Commissioning one use against shared fire systems still serving incomplete floors
- Closing residual risk with a single-use defects list
